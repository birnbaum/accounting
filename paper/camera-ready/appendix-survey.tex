\subsection{Survey Scope and Provider Disclosures}
\label{app:inventory}
We reviewed publicly available environmental disclosures from 50 cloud providers across general-purpose compute, GPU/HPC (neocloud), CDN/edge, application hosting, and object storage.
The sample includes major cloud platforms and selected regional and specialized providers, with a focus on Europe and North America.
We excluded colocation-only providers, general business SaaS, model-only APIs, and independent carbon-accounting tools.
Providers were selected regardless of their environmental disclosures.
We initially reviewed official documentation and reports on 27 September 2026.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Classification.} Categories reflect providers' publicly available documentation, reports, and claims.
We did not test every customer reporting tool or independently verify all reported figures.
Each provider is assigned the first applicable category below.
\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \textcolor{surveyReport}{\textbf{Customer report}} (15): emissions attributed to an individual customer's usage, excluding reports that show only estimated savings.
\item \textcolor{surveyMetrics}{\textbf{Public metrics}} (17): quantified corporate or operational environmental results, without a per-customer report.
\item \textcolor{surveyClaims}{\textbf{Claims only}} (12): environmental commitments, practices, targets, or projected savings, without quantified results.
\item \textcolor{surveyMissing}{\textbf{Not found}} (6): no qualifying disclosure identified in the reviewed sources.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Coverage and Granularity of Cloud Emissions Reports}
\label{app:reporting-coverage}
Of the 50 surveyed providers, 15 claim to offer reports that attribute emissions to individual customers.
Table~\ref{tab:reporting-coverage} compares their reporting intervals, the breakdowns available to customers, and the emissions sources and electricity accounting methods covered by their reports.
The entries are based on the public documentation cited beside each provider. We additionally checked the documentation for reporting breakdowns on 6 October 2026.
The columns should be read as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Reporting interval} gives the shortest period for which a provider documents emissions totals. We did not test all dashboards, but daily reporting in particular warrants caution. For example, in our June~2026 tests, Scaleway's daily reports appeared unreliable.
\item \textbf{Reporting breakdown} lists how customers can split or filter reported emissions. Detail varies by service and reporting interface. The listed dimensions are not necessarily available together.
\item The \textbf{Scopes 1, 2, and 3} columns show which emissions providers account for when calculating the footprints allocated to customers. Scope~1 covers direct emissions, such as fuel combustion. The two Scope~2 columns distinguish methods for calculating emissions from electricity use. \emph{Location-based} estimates use the average emissions per unit of electricity supplied by the local grid. \emph{Market-based} estimates use emission factors associated with the provider's electricity purchases, including contracts and renewable energy certificates. These columns also cover electricity classified as provider Scope~3, such as colocation electricity. The five Scope~3 columns compare coverage of selected emissions sources. They do not correspond one-to-one to GHG Protocol categories.
\end{itemize}

We did not independently verify the reported emissions.
Coverage may vary by service and region.
A checkmark indicates inclusion but does not imply complete coverage of an emissions source.
Customer allocation methods and their responses to engineering actions are assessed separately in Table~\ref{tab:levers} and Appendix~\ref{app:action-evidence}.

\subsection{Interpreting the Action Classifications}
\label{app:action-evidence}
The following paragraphs explain the five action classifications in Table~\ref{tab:levers}, which covers the 11 providers offering customer reports for general-purpose compute.

% These headings already end with a question mark.
\renewcommand{\parag}[1]{\vspace{2mm}\noindent\textbf{#1}\hspace{2mm}}

\parag{Efficient resource: Does using more energy-efficient hardware for the same work lower reported emissions?}
Most methods use prices or pooled resource weights, which need not track the efficiency of the hardware running a workload.
GCP weights SKU usage by list price~\cite{schneider-mattia-2024}, Azure uses cost normalized to exclude discounts~\cite{microsoft-s3-2021,azure-methodology-2026}, and Oracle's spend-based method uses undiscounted charges~\cite{oracle-emissions-management}.
AWS EC2 allocates service totals in proportion to normalized instance-hours~\cite{aws-ccft-model3}, and IBM VPC uses vCPU-hours~\cite{ibm-carbon-calc-v3}, without retaining the energy use of the customer's particular machine.
Alibaba allocates by cloud resource quantity without specifying how its weights account for hardware efficiency~\cite{alibaba-cloud-carbon-footprint}.
OVHcloud models dedicated servers individually but averages hardware impacts within each shared-instance range~\cite{ovh-eit-v2}, while Exoscale averages across each product's hardware pool~\cite{exoscale-impact-methodology}.
Scaleway models CPU-instance power using processor characteristics and CPU utilization, but uses average consumption estimates for GPU instances~\cite{scaleway-instances-footprint}.
T~Cloud maps services to infrastructure components using power profiles derived from measurements, so its estimates depend on the modeled configuration~\cite{tcloud-sustainability-release,tcloud-sustainability-dashboard}.
OUTSCALE does not disclose its customer allocation weights~\cite{outscale-carbon-methodology}.

\parag{Lower utilization: Does lower utilization reduce reported emissions when reserved capacity and runtime stay the same?}
Scaleway's CPU-instance reports use actual CPU utilization in the power model, so reduced activity can lower attributed electricity use, whereas its GPU estimates do not follow customer activity~\cite{scaleway-instances-footprint}.
For AWS EC2~\cite{aws-ccft-model3}, GCP Compute Engine~\cite{schneider-mattia-2024}, IBM VPC~\cite{ibm-carbon-calc-v3} and Exoscale~\cite{exoscale-impact-methodology}, lower CPU activity leaves the reserved quantities used for allocation unchanged.
Azure's normalized-cost weight likewise stays unchanged for the same VM and runtime~\cite{microsoft-s3-2021,azure-methodology-2026}.
OVHcloud estimates server energy at full load and allocates VM footprints by size and billed hours, without using the customer's actual CPU activity~\cite{ovh-eit-v2}.
T~Cloud's release notes describe allocation by provisioned resources~\cite{tcloud-sustainability-release}, while its blog describes allocation by actual utilization~\cite{tcloud-sustainability-dashboard}, without explaining how the two are combined.
Oracle~\cite{oracle-emissions-management}, Alibaba~\cite{alibaba-cloud-carbon-footprint} and OUTSCALE~\cite{outscale-carbon-methodology} do not specify whether customer CPU activity changes the allocation at a fixed reservation.

\parag{Right-size: Does reserving less capacity for the same work and runtime lower reported emissions?}
Smaller reservations reduce the quantities used for allocation in most methods.
Within a comparable instance family, this reduces normalized instance-hours for AWS EC2~\cite{aws-ccft-model3}, billable resource units for GCP~\cite{schneider-mattia-2024}, vCPU-hours for IBM VPC~\cite{ibm-carbon-calc-v3}, and reserved-resource shares for OVHcloud~\cite{ovh-eit-v2} and Exoscale~\cite{exoscale-impact-methodology}.
Scaleway allocates manufacturing impacts by reserved resource shares, so smaller reservations reduce this component, but the total also depends on CPU utilization after resizing~\cite{scaleway-instances-footprint}.
Azure's cost-based allocation rewards smaller VMs when they reduce normalized cost~\cite{microsoft-s3-2021}, and Oracle's spend-based method similarly rewards lower undiscounted charges~\cite{oracle-emissions-management}.
T~Cloud uses provisioned resources in its calculation, but its documentation does not establish how increased utilization after resizing affects the result~\cite{tcloud-sustainability-release,tcloud-sustainability-dashboard}.
Alibaba does not specify whether its resource quantities measure reserved capacity or actual activity~\cite{alibaba-cloud-carbon-footprint}, and OUTSCALE does not disclose the allocation weights needed to assess resizing~\cite{outscale-carbon-methodology}.

\parag{Region: Does using the same amount of electricity in a cleaner region lower reported emissions?}
Most providers publish location-based figures and use regional emission factors, including AWS~\cite{aws-ccft-model3}, GCP~\cite{schneider-mattia-2024}, Oracle~\cite{oracle-emissions-management}, OVHcloud~\cite{ovh-eit-v2}, Scaleway~\cite{scaleway-calculation-values} and Exoscale~\cite{exoscale-impact-methodology}.
The same electricity use is therefore attributed fewer emissions in a cleaner region.
Oracle provides this comparison through its power-based method, while its spend-based method is market-based only~\cite{oracle-emissions-management}.
IBM also documents location-based reporting, but currently lists that output as temporarily disabled~\cite{ibm-carbon-current}.
OUTSCALE's calculation likewise scales electricity emissions with the grid factor, although both currently covered regions use France's factor~\cite{outscale-carbon-methodology}.
Azure~\cite{azure-carbon-data} and T~Cloud~\cite{tcloud-sustainability-release} expose only market-based Scope~2 figures, while Alibaba includes green-electricity adjustments without documenting a separate location-based result~\cite{alibaba-cloud-carbon-footprint}.
Their regional totals may differ, but do not show how differences in grid carbon intensity affect reported emissions.

\parag{Time: Does running the same workload at cleaner times lower reported emissions?}
%We consider shifts within the same month, with total resource-hours unchanged.
None of the reviewed methods documents a reduction in a customer's reported emissions from shifting work to cleaner hours.
GCP computes internal emissions hourly, but its published customer formula applies aggregate SKU and regional factors to customer usage without matching that usage to individual hours~\cite{schneider-mattia-2024}.
AWS allocates monthly service emissions in proportion to customer usage within that month~\cite{aws-ccft-model3}.
OVHcloud~\cite{ovh-eit-v2} and OUTSCALE~\cite{outscale-carbon-methodology} use yearly electricity emission factors.
IBM generally uses source factors updated yearly and applies them for up to a year~\cite{ibm-carbon-calc-v3}.
Scaleway~\cite{scaleway-calculation-values} and Exoscale~\cite{exoscale-impact-methodology} do not specify whether their electricity emission factors represent monthly or yearly averages.
Azure~\cite{azure-carbon-data}, T~Cloud~\cite{tcloud-sustainability-release} and Alibaba~\cite{alibaba-cloud-carbon-footprint} lack the separate location-based output needed for this comparison.
Oracle does not disclose the time resolution of its electricity factors or how customers' execution times enter the allocation~\cite{oracle-emissions-management}.
