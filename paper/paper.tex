\begin{abstract}
Organizations seeking to reduce the carbon footprint of their cloud applications must rely on two largely disconnected ways of measuring it, each with important limitations.
Bottom-up metrics like Software Carbon Intensity (\SCI) provide signals for carbon-aware optimization, but tenants lack the information needed to account for many provider-side overheads.
Top-down reports by cloud providers provide a more comprehensive view of a tenant's carbon footprint but are coarse and methodologically opaque.
Because the two approaches differ in scope and reporting frequency, the current state of the art treats them as decoupled: bottom-up metrics for optimization, top-down reports for corporate disclosure.

We argue that the two signals should be \emph{reconcilable}.
A per-work\-load metric whose improvements never surface in the provider's audited report will not be adopted for accountability at scale.
We survey the state of the art in cloud carbon accounting and propose a vision for reconciled \SCI (\RSCI): a per-workload metric that re-anchors a bottom-up energy estimate to the cloud provider's top-down report through a residual.
By decomposing and allocating this residual according to its physical drivers, \RSCI can preserve operational incentives while also attributing idle capacity and embodied carbon to the workloads that drive them.
We show that today's top-down reporting is still too coarse and methodologically inconsistent to support credible reconciliation and lay out concrete steps in provider reporting and standards to make it practical.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A major challenge in sustainable computing is quantifying the carbon footprint of workloads.
It is a prerequisite for determining the effectiveness of carbon-reduction strategies, for reporting to regulators and investors, and for making informed decisions about workload scheduling and adaptation.
Two largely separate approaches exist: bottom-up modeling and top-down corporate greenhouse gas (GHG) reporting.

\parag{Bottom-up metrics}
Most carbon-aware computing research has focused on optimizing bottom-up metrics like operational carbon~\cite{radovanovic2022google,hanafy2024gaia,sukprasert2024limits,gsteiger2024caribou,murillo2024cdn,lechowicz2023pause,hanafy2024carbonscaler,wiesner2025qualitytime,wiesner2025moving}: the estimated emissions from a workload's marginal increase in energy consumption.
This signal is straightforward to obtain but does not capture overheads from idle hardware or embodied emissions.
To provide a more comprehensive metric, the Green Software Foundation proposed the Software Carbon Intensity (\SCI) specification~\cite{sci-spec}, codified as ISO/IEC~21031 in 2024, which expresses the carbon emitted per unit of work and folds in an embodied term.
However, Bashir et al.~\cite{bashir2024} show that \SCI can produce perverse incentives for workload placement and scaling in practice.
Furthermore, every bottom-up metric, \SCI included, can only capture what a tenant can observe or model.

\parag{Top-down reporting}
Due to regulatory pressure, cloud providers increasingly offer customer-facing dashboards and reports enabling tenants to estimate and disclose their cloud carbon footprint in line with the GHG Protocol~\cite{ghg-protocol-corp}.
While the reporting boundaries and allocation methods can differ significantly by provider, the general methodology is usually public and sometimes even externally audited.
However, the reported numbers are highly aggregated:
reports mostly contain a single number per month, region, customer, and GHG Protocol scope, sometimes coarsely categorized further into service categories.
The reports are typically published about 14-21 days after month-end, which makes them comprehensive but unsuitable for any type of real-time optimization.

\parag{The need for reconcilability}
Today, when an engineering team improves the bottom-up metric of an internal workload, it is entirely unclear whether that improvement reaches the provider's top-down report.
The two can diverge in both directions.
The top-down report contains overheads that bottom-up models cannot cover without internal provider data.
And conversely, the actions a bottom-up metric does capture, such as lowering a VM's utilization and thus its power draw, may not move the reported number at all, if the provider allocates carbon to tenants by usage-hours or revenue instead of measured energy.

The Green Software Foundation explicitly endorses this separation, distinguishing ``measuring for action'' (per-workload signals that drive engineering) from ``measuring for reporting'' (corporate carbon accounting) and emphasizing that the two serve different goals and should not be conflated~\cite{gsf-measuring}.
\emph{We argue that different purposes should not mean disconnected accounting.}
Regulatory disclosure requirements~\cite{eu2022csrd} create a need for workload-level signals that attribute reported cloud emissions across internal teams and products.
A per-workload metric whose gains never land in official reporting will not be trusted and adopted for accountability at scale.

\parag{Contributions}
We argue that bottom-up carbon metrics should \emph{reconcile} with the provider's top-down GHG report by construction.
Towards this, we make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item We review the state of the art in cloud carbon accounting, including a survey of 50 providers and a matrix showing which engineering actions are reflected in providers' documented accounting methods.
\item We introduce \emph{reconciled Software Carbon Intensity} (\RSCI), which anchors a bottom-up energy estimate to the provider's top-down report through a \emph{residual}, so per-workload emissions sum to the reported total by construction.
\item We decompose the residual into energy-driven and capacity-driven components and propose allocating them according to their drivers, so \RSCI can account not only for the energy a workload consumes, but also for the idle capacity and embodied hardware the workload contributes to.
\item We diagnose why today's provider reports are too coarse and methodologically opaque for computing credible \RSCI scores in practice, and set out concrete asks on reporting and standards.
\end{itemize}

 \section{Bottom-Up Metrics Miss Provider Overheads}
\label{sec:background}

A bottom-up metric estimates a workload's carbon from quantities a tenant can measure without access to providers' internal data.
The de facto choice in most research papers is operational carbon, which we write as \OSCI (operational software carbon intensity) to match the terminology in Bashir et al.~\cite{bashir2024}:
\[
\OSCI \;=\; \frac{\E \cdot \I}{\R},
\]
the workload's energy $\E$ times its region's carbon intensity $\I$ (in gCO$_2$/kWh), per functional unit of work $\R$ (an API call, a training run, a GB stored per month).\footnote{We write CO$_2$ or ``carbon'' as shorthand for CO$_2$-equivalent emissions throughout.}
It is straightforward, transparent, and it responds to the two operational dimensions a tenant controls directly: energy efficiency and carbon-aware timing or placement.
But it says nothing about hardware efficiency, i.e., whether the workload requires less provisioned capacity.

To capture this missing dimension, the Software Carbon Intensity (\SCI) specification (ISO/IEC~21031~\cite{sci-spec}) adds an embodied term:
\[
\SCI \;=\; \frac{\E \cdot \I \;+\; \M}{\R},
\]
where $\M$ is the workload's amortized share of the hardware's embodied carbon, allocated according to its reserved time and resource share as described by Gupta et al.~\cite{gupta2022act}.
The specification defines three categories of \emph{action} for reducing software carbon: (i) energy efficiency, (ii) hardware efficiency, and (iii) carbon awareness, and requires the score to fall whenever a tenant takes any of these actions~\cite{sci-spec}.
\OSCI already responds to the first and third through \E and \I, the embodied term \M is what extends \SCI to the second.

However, Bashir et al.~\cite{bashir2024} identify a \emph{sunk-carbon fallacy} when past embodied emissions guide operational scheduling.
A lower amortized embodied term can favor a less energy-efficient server, even though scheduling cannot undo its manufacturing emissions.
They therefore recommend sticking to \OSCI for operational decisions such as scheduling and scaling.
But \OSCI is incomplete: it rewards neither reserving less hardware (beyond the energy it saves), nor purchasing less and lower-embodied hardware.

Most importantly, \emph{bottom-up estimates do not guarantee complete attribution of provider emissions}.
Tools and services such as Cloud Carbon Footprint~\cite{cloudcarbonfootprint}, BoaviztAPI~\cite{simon2024boaviztapi}, and Greenpixie~\cite{greenpixie-cloud-methodology} help tenants estimate operational and embodied emissions, but accounting for shared idle capacity and other provider-side overheads requires information that tenants cannot directly observe.
Even operational estimates can disagree with provider allocations: Jacquet et al.~\cite{jacquet2025cinergy} estimate up to three times as much carbon for busy VMs as OVHcloud's method, which allocates emissions by VM size.
Adding an embodied term does not resolve these gaps or establish consistency with the provider's inventory.

\section{Top-Down Reports Respond Inconsistently to Carbon-Reduction Actions}
\label{sec:sota}

To support customer emissions disclosure, cloud providers have moved beyond annual corporate reports toward customer-facing tools that attribute emissions to individual tenants.
But a top-down report is only useful for engineering if it responds to the same actions a per-workload metric rewards.
We surveyed 50 cloud providers in September~2026, spanning general-purpose compute, GPU/HPC, CDN/edge, application hosting, and object storage.
Interestingly, none of the 10 GPU/HPC (neocloud) providers we surveyed expose per-tenant emissions.
Appendix~\ref{app:inventory} gives the full provider list, inclusion criteria, and source evidence.
Table~\ref{tab:levers} compares the eleven surveyed providers with customer carbon reporting for general-purpose compute, showing which actions their methods reflect.
Appendix~\ref{app:action-evidence} explains the classification.

We find that no provider responds to all actions, and the same action can be reflected inconsistently across providers’ reports.
This is because a top-down report is built in three steps: providers decide \emph{what} emissions to count, \emph{allocate} the datacenter total down to services and tenants, and \emph{report} the result at a chosen granularity.
In practice, methodologies diverge at each step.

\parag{What cloud providers cover}
Providers decompose their total datacenter emissions over the reporting period along the GHG Protocol's three scopes.
\emph{Scope~1}, direct on-site emissions such as backup-diesel combustion and refrigerant leakage, is usually negligible for most tenants today, though the increasing deployment of behind-the-meter gas generation at datacenters may change this in future.
\emph{Scope~2} covers emissions from purchased electricity.\footnote{The GHG Protocol mandates dual reporting for Scope~2: \emph{location-based} figures reflect actual grid emissions, while \emph{market-based} figures net out procurement instruments like power purchase agreements (PPAs) and renewable energy certificates (RECs), which in practice often moves the market-based number to near zero. Since engineering teams have no control over the provider's procurement choices, the market-based figure is not actionable for workload optimization, which is why \SCI explicitly excludes it~\cite{sci-spec}. We only consider the location-based figure in this paper.}
\emph{Scope~3} covers amortized cradle-to-gate hardware and, depending on the provider, building embodied carbon, upstream fuel-and-transmission losses (FERA), freight, and end-of-life.
No two of the three hyperscalers count the same set of Scope-3 categories.
For example, only GCP counts employee business travel and commuting~\cite{google-carbon-methodology}, only Azure counts hardware end-of-life~\cite{microsoft-s3-2021}, and building-embodied carbon is counted by AWS and GCP but not Azure~\cite{aws-ccft-model3,google-carbon-methodology}.
Appendix~\ref{app:reporting-coverage} compares the documented emissions coverage, electricity accounting methods, and reporting detail across the surveyed providers offering customer reports.

\parag{How cloud providers allocate}
Providers then allocate the datacenter total to tenants in proportion to their share of an \emph{activity unit}.
The units are inconsistent and lean on economics to differing degrees.
For example, AWS allocates foundational services by usage units, including normalized instance-hours for EC2, and non-foundational services by ``equivalent revenue''~\cite{aws-ccft-model3}.
GCP distributes each product's energy across SKUs in proportion to list price before allocating by per-SKU usage such as vCPU-hours~\cite{schneider-mattia-2024}.
This choice is at least transparent and predictable: a tenant can reliably reduce its allocation by shifting to cheaper SKUs, even if it usually cannot tell which SKU is actually more energy-efficient.

Oracle describes allocating measured hardware energy to workloads without specifying the customer weights~\cite{oracle-emissions-management}.
Scaleway models CPU-instance power from utilization~\cite{scaleway-instances-footprint}, while T~Cloud uses service models calibrated from infrastructure measurements~\cite{tcloud-sustainability-release,tcloud-sustainability-dashboard}.
Scaleway's bare-metal methodology~\cite{scaleway-baremetal-footprint} is a clean example: because the customer controls a dedicated physical server, Scaleway can attribute that server’s operational energy and amortized manufacturing footprint directly to the customer.

The activity unit also decides the \emph{efficient-resource} action: switching to a more efficient instance lowers the number only where the per-unit factor is grounded in energy or LCA, whereas at GCP it tracks list price, so a cheaper-but-dirtier SKU can look greener~\cite{schneider-mattia-2024}.

\parag{How cloud providers report}
Most customer dashboards report at the service level (for GCP, ``Compute Engine'', ``Networking'', ...), far coarser than a bottom-up metric's per-workload view.
A tenant sees Scope 1, 2, and 3 totals per service $\times$ region $\times$ month, released 14 to 21 days after the period closes.
Azure also offers finer resource-level breakdowns~\cite{azure-carbon-overview}.
AWS and GCP's monthly allocation rules erase \emph{when} a tenant runs: shifting the same work to cleaner hours does not lower its usage-based share of monthly emissions, even though GCP computes on hourly carbon intensity internally~\cite{aws-ccft-model3,schneider-mattia-2024}.

Providers also differ on \emph{how} Scope~2 is valued: AWS, GCP, and most others expose a location-based figure that reflects the regional grid, so a cleaner region lowers emissions for the same electricity use.
Azure, T~Cloud, and Alibaba Cloud instead publish market-based or ``green-power-adjusted'' figures without a separate location-based Scope~2 view~\cite{azure-carbon-data,tcloud-sustainability-dashboard,alibaba-cloud-carbon-footprint}, obscuring the grid signal behind the region action.

\section{Reconciling Bottom-Up and Top-Down}
\label{sec:rsci}

We introduce \emph{reconciled} \SCI (\RSCI), a family of per-workload metrics that anchors bottom-up carbon estimates to a provider's top-down report.
Its purpose is \emph{attributional}: assigning the reported emissions, including shared overheads, to workloads.
The residual closes the gap between these views, so attributed workload emissions sum to the reported total by construction.
\SCI alone does not guarantee this consistency.
This makes disagreements explicit without treating either estimate as ground truth.

\emph{Consequential} analysis asks how emissions change as a result of an intervention.
El-Zahr and Zilberman~\cite{elzahr2025measurement} make this distinction in accounting for network traffic.
Building on demand-aware attribution in Fair-CO$_2$~\cite{han2025fairco2}, we propose allocating energy- and capacity-driven residual components according to the activities and provisioning requirements that drive them.

\parag{Setup}
Let $i$ index a \emph{workload}: a distinct billable unit a tenant runs, like a service or a batch job.
A reporting \emph{slice} is the provider's finest published granularity for one tenant (service $\times$ region $\times$ month).
A workload can span multiple slices (e.g., multiple services or regions).
Rewriting \OSCI and \SCI using this per-workload notation we get
\begin{equation}
\OSCI_i \;=\; \frac{\sum_s \E_{i,s}\cdot\I_s}{\R_i}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\SCI_i \;=\; \frac{\sum_s \E_{i,s}\cdot\I_s + \M_i}{\R_i},
\label{eq:osci-sci-repetition}
\end{equation}
where $\E_{i,s}$ is the workload's modeled energy within the slice, $\I_s$ the slice's location-based carbon intensity, $\M_i$ the workload machine's embodied term, and $\R_i$ its total functional output over the period.

\OSCI, \SCI, and \RSCI all rely on a bottom-up energy estimate $\E_{i,s}$.
We can, for example, derive $\E_{i,s}$ from per-resource quantities,
\begin{equation}
\E_{i,s} \;=\; \mathrm{PUE}\cdot\sum_{p} q_{i,s,p} \cdot \varepsilon_p,
\label{eq:bu-energy}
\end{equation}
where $p$ indexes resources (vCPU-hours, GPU-hours, GB-hours of storage, I/O ops, network GB),
$q_{i,s,p}$ is workload $i$'s measured consumption of resource $p$ within slice $s$,
$\varepsilon_p$ is a per-type energy coefficient from sources such as BoaviztAPI~\cite{simon2024boaviztapi} or the Cloud Carbon Footprint project~\cite{cloudcarbonfootprint}.
$\mathrm{PUE}$ (power usage effectiveness) is an optional factor for modeling facility overhead.

\parag{Reconciliation}
A tenant's bottom-up model cannot capture all of Scope~2: energy coefficients are approximate, shared idle capacity is unobservable, and a fixed PUE ignores that real facility overhead varies with season and weather.
Emissions in Scope 1 and 3 are even less observable.
Therefore, for each slice, we define the \emph{residual} emissions as the difference between the provider's top-down reported total carbon footprint $C^{\downarrow}_{s}$ and the bottom-up estimate:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{s} \;=\; C^{\downarrow}_{s} \;-\; \sum_i \E_{i,s}\,\I_s .
\label{eq:residual-total}
\end{equation}
\RSCI distributes this residual across the slice's workloads by weights.
It uses the same operational term as \OSCI, but adds a residual term, taken over one or more \emph{residual components} $\Delta^{c}_s$ (with $\sum_c \Delta^{c}_s = \Delta_s$), each distributed across the slice's workloads by weights $w^{c}_{i,s}$ that sum to one ($\sum_{i \in s} w^{c}_{i,s} = 1$):
\begin{equation}
\RSCI_i \;=\; \frac{\sum_s \bigl( \overbrace{\E_{i,s}\cdot\I_s}^\text{operational term} \;+\; \overbrace{\textstyle\sum_c w^{c}_{i,s}\,\Delta^{c}_{s}}^\text{residual term}\;\;\;\bigr)}{\R_i}.
\label{eq:rsci}
\end{equation}
Attributed carbon then reconciles by construction: scaling each score back to carbon by its $\R_i$, the workload attributions sum to the reported total across slices,
\begin{equation}
\textstyle\sum_i \RSCI_i\,\R_i
=
\textstyle\sum_s
\left(
\sum_{i \in s} \E_{i,s}\I_s
+
\Delta_s
\right)
=
\textstyle\sum_s C^\downarrow_s .
\label{eq:reconcile}
\end{equation}
A workload spanning several slices sums its per-slice attributions, and since each balances on its own, reconciliation holds across slices.
This identity holds for \emph{any} partition of the residual into components and \emph{any} normalized weights.

\parag{Decomposing the residual}
The simplest allocation choice would be to split the full residual $\Delta_s$ evenly, giving each of the slice's $N_s$ workloads a share $w_i = 1/N_s$.
This reconciles, but ignores differences in workloads' energy use and capacity requirements.
To improve this, we first decompose the residual the way the provider reports it, by GHG scope.
Let $C_{s}^{\downarrow\text{S1}}$, $C_{s}^{\downarrow\text{S2}}$, $C_{s}^{\downarrow\text{S3}}$ denote the top-down reported per-slice, per-scope emissions.
Since our bottom-up model estimates only operational Scope~2 carbon, the per-scope residuals (summing to $\Delta_s$) are
\begin{equation}
\Delta^{\text{S1}}_{s} = C^{\downarrow\text{S1}}_{s}, \qquad
\Delta^{\text{S2}}_{s} = C^{\downarrow\text{S2}}_{s} - \sum_i \E_{i,s}\,\I_s, \qquad
\Delta^{\text{S3}}_{s} = C^{\downarrow\text{S3}}_{s},
\label{eq:residual-scopes}
\end{equation}
$\Delta^{\text{S1}}$ absorbs diesel and refrigerants.
$\Delta^{\text{S2}}$ includes unmodeled idle energy, facility overhead, and energy-model differences.
$\Delta^{\text{S3}}$ includes embodied hardware and the rest of Scope~3.

The scope split mirrors the provider's reporting but not the actions a tenant controls.
What matters for incentives is whether an emission scales with \emph{energy usage} (i.e., the bottom-up model) or with \emph{provisioned capacity} (i.e., idle power and embodied emissions).
We therefore propose regrouping the three scope residuals into an energy-driven $\Delta^{\text{en}}_{s}$ and a capacity-driven $\Delta^{\text{cap}}_{s}$ term, writing $\beta^{k}\in[0,1]$ for the energy-driven fraction of scope $k$'s residual:
\begin{equation}
\Delta^{\text{en}}_{s} = \sum_{k} \beta^{k}\,\Delta^{k}_{s}, \qquad
\Delta^{\text{cap}}_{s} = \sum_{k} (1-\beta^{k})\,\Delta^{k}_{s}.
\label{eq:residual-levers}
\end{equation}
The betas must be estimated by tenants from the provider's methodology.
For a positive residual, a larger $\beta$ shifts weight from capacity requirements to energy use.
As a starting point, we treat Scope~1 as capacity-driven ($\beta^{\text{S1}}=0$).
Scope~2 mixes energy-driven terms (unmodeled overhead, energy-model differences) with capacity-driven ones (idle power), so $\beta^{\text{S2}}$ depends on how well the bottom-up model captures the energy-driven part.
Scope~3 combines capacity-driven terms (embodied hardware and buildings) with energy-driven terms (upstream electricity, i.e.\ FERA).

Since $\Delta^{\text{en}}_s + \Delta^{\text{cap}}_s = \Delta_s$, any $\beta$ preserves reconciliation.
The resulting incentives also depend on residual signs, allocation weights, and how the provider's reported total responds to an action.

\parag{Energy allocation}
The energy-driven residual $\Delta^{\text{en}}$ should scale proportionally with the operational term.
At fixed slice intensity $\I_s$, we use the normalized weight
\begin{equation}
w^{\text{en}}_{i,s} = \frac{\E_{i,s}\cdot\I_s}{\sum_{j \in s} \E_{j,s}\cdot\I_s} = \frac{\E_{i,s}}{\sum_{j \in s} \E_{j,s}}.
\label{eq:w-energy}
\end{equation}
With time-varying carbon intensity, replace $\E_{i,s}\I_s$ by time-summed operational emissions in both the bottom-up term and these weights, retaining the reporting slice for reconciliation.

\parag{Capacity allocation}
We propose allocating the capacity-driven residual $\Delta^{\text{cap}}$ according to workloads' provisioning requirements.
For example, (i) demand peaks require providers to hold capacity that is idle outside peak periods, (ii) availability requirements call for redundant capacity~\cite{eilam2021transparent}, and (iii) latency constraints may require warm instances or spare capacity to avoid startup and queuing delays.
Allocation weights should reflect each workload’s contribution to these capacity needs.

Related work provides starting points for defining these weights.
Islam and Ren allocate static non-IT energy equally among active tenants and dynamic energy in proportion to their IT energy use~\cite{islam2016energyaccounting}.
Similarly, Sharma and Fuerst allocate idle energy equally among active functions but control-plane energy by invocation count~\cite{sharma2024accountable_footprint_serverless,sharma2025disaggregation}.
For capacity driven by peaks, Han et al. use Shapley-based attribution to link embodied carbon to peak demand~\cite{han2024gametheoretic,han2025fairco2}, and Schien et al. assign more fixed network energy to traffic during peak periods~\cite{schien2025causal}.
Choosing among such rules requires considering fairness among tenants~\cite{westerhof2023allocation,montoyafranco2025fairwatt}, optimization incentives~\cite{li2025llmserving}, and the stability of demand responses highlighted by peak-based pricing~\cite{nasiriani2016peakpricing}.
Adapting these approaches to residual components and evaluating the resulting incentives remain future work.

\parag{Online use of \RSCI}
Exact \RSCI scores are retrospective as the residual $\Delta_s$ is only known once the provider publishes its top-down report.
During the period, tenants can still optimize against provisional scores based on historical reports and load forecasts.
These scores are then adjusted to match the final \RSCI values once the report arrives.
Validating these estimates and quantifying their uncertainty remain future work.

\section{Example}
\label{sec:demo}

We illustrate \RSCI's reconciliation and incentives.
Using an assumed provider report, we model a single workload on a single node, reporting carbon per token served. Results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:demo}.
Code and data for reproducing the experiment and Figure~\ref{fig:demo} are available at \url{https://github.com/birnbaum/rsci}.

\parag{Scenario}
We model an internal LLM service on an 8$\times$H100 node over three days, reporting hourly emissions in gCO$_2$ per million tokens (gCO$_2$/Mtok).
We model power as linear between $2.5$\,kW idle and the rated maximum of $10.2$\,kW~\cite{nvidia-dgx-h100-datasheet} and we assume a PUE of $1.2$.
We model load using the 2024 Azure LLM inference trace~\cite{stojkovic2025dynamollm} and carbon intensity using California (CAISO) data from Electricity Maps~\cite{electricitymaps2026}.
The shared pool follows a synthetic daily load cycle of approximately 30--90\,\% of available capacity, independent of the workload trace.
Retrospectively, the provider reports the node's footprint top-down: $C^{\downarrow\text{S2}}\!=\!80$\,kg CO$_2$ for Scope~2 and $C^{\downarrow\text{S3}}\!=\!20$\,kg for Scope~3 (we omit Scope~1 for simplicity).
We ask how well the aggregated carbon-per-token metrics reconcile to that $100$\,kg total, and what behaviour they reward along the way.

\parag{\OSCI}
This operational metric counts only the marginal energy the work draws above idle, scaled by the PUE and the hourly carbon intensity.
It rewards the main actions a tenant controls in this scenario: using less energy (\E) and computing work during cleaner hours (\I).
By construction the node's idle power and its embodied hardware are not considered.
Lacking any embodied term, it cannot be misguided by depreciated hardware (i.e., the sunk-carbon fallacy~\cite{bashir2024}).
\OSCI reports between 52 and 270\,gCO$_2$/Mtok (${\sim}5.2\times$ range) and attributes about 58\,kg in total.

\parag{\SCI}
\SCI adds an amortized embodied term to the same operational energy estimate used for \OSCI.\footnote{We assume the node's cradle-to-gate embodied carbon is $2500$\,kg: NVIDIA reports $1312$\,kg for the 8-GPU HGX~H100 baseboard~\cite{nvidia2025hgxh100pcf} alone. We assume the node is amortised over a 4-year lifespan, giving $5.1$\,kg over three days, equivalent to $12.4$\,gCO$_2$/Mtok across the $415.4$ million tokens served.}
Over the three days, this adds $5.1$\,kg, bringing \SCI to $63.0$\,kg, which still falls short of the provider's $100$\,kg report.

\parag{\RSCI}
\RSCI keeps \OSCI's operational term and adds the residual, the gap between the provider's reported total and the bottom-up estimate.
Following \S\ref{sec:rsci}, we split the residual into an energy-driven part, $\Delta_s^{\text{en}}\!\approx\!12.2$\,kg, and a capacity-driven part, $\Delta_s^{\text{cap}}\!\approx\!29.9$\,kg.
For illustration, we use $\beta^{\text{S2}}\!=\!0.1$ and $\beta^{\text{S3}}\!=\!0.5$.

The energy-driven residual is allocated in proportion to modeled operational emissions.
For the capacity-driven residual, we use a simple rule inspired by Schien et al.~\cite{schien2025causal} that gives more weight to work during busy pool hours.
The hourly weights are $Q_h s_h^2/\sum_t Q_t s_t^2$, where $Q_h$ is tokens served and $s_h$ is aggregate pool demand divided by fixed available capacity.

The energy-driven residual adds 11--57\,gCO$_2$/Mtok (${\sim}5.2\times$ range).
The capacity-driven residual contributes $16$--$139$\,gCO$_2$/Mtok, compared with $72$ under uniform allocation per token.
By construction, multiplying each hourly \RSCI score by its token count and summing gives the reported $100$\,kg.
Compared with uniform allocation, this rule attributes more capacity emissions per token during shared peaks.
Shifting flexible work to quieter hours can reduce future capacity needs when peaks determine provisioning.

\parag{Comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:properties} summarizes the accounting properties and conditional incentives of \RSCI:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
  \item \emph{Rewards operational actions.} Its operational term responds to energy savings and cleaner hours or regions.
  These improvements reduce the reconciled total \emph{if the provider's report reflects them}.
  Appendix~\ref{app:operational-incentives} gives the conditions for individual workload incentives.
  \item \emph{Attributes embodied carbon.} Past hardware purchases are a sunk cost and should not steer operational choices~\cite{bashir2024}, but ignoring embodied carbon omits part of the reported footprint and drops an incentive to provision less hardware.
  \item \emph{Avoids the sunk-carbon fallacy~\cite{bashir2024}.} Allocation by energy and capacity requirements gives no discount for depreciated hardware within a reporting slice.
  Fleet age can still affect the provider's inventory.
  \item \emph{Allocates residuals by their drivers.} We propose allocating energy residuals by operational emissions and capacity residuals by provisioning needs.
  Defining and validating suitable capacity weights remains future work.
  The incentive conditions are given in Appendix~\ref{app:operational-incentives}.
  \item \emph{Reconciles by construction.} Of the three metrics, only \RSCI guarantees that attributed workload emissions sum to the provider's reported total.
\end{itemize}

\section{Barriers to Practical Adoption}
\label{sec:practice}

Reconciliation alone is not sufficient for trustworthy accounting.
In practice, computing credible \RSCI scores requires provider reports that are sufficiently detailed, transparent, and standardized.

To evaluate whether this is possible today, we attempted to compute \RSCI scores using production accounts on AWS and Azure, as well as test deployments on Scaleway.
We identify three barriers to obtaining the information needed for reconciliation.

\parag{Top-down reports are too coarse}
\RSCI's per-workload attribution is straightforward when a reporting slice contains a single workload, as in \S\ref{sec:demo}.
In practice, however, providers aggregate many workloads into a small number of monthly service categories.
The coarser the report, across both resources and time, the more attribution depends on heuristic allocation that cannot be checked against the reported data.

We observed this on six months of production data from an AWS account running a web service in Frankfurt (December~2025--May~2026).
Although AWS bills individual services separately, its carbon report aggregates most emissions into a service category called ``Other'', see Figure~\ref{fig:aws}.
Across the six months, this category accounts for 67--96\,\% of reported emissions and combines diverse services such as load balancing, data transfer, control-plane operations, and public IPv4 addresses.
The report provides no resource-level breakdown to validate attribution to individual workloads.

\begin{callforaction}
Providers already meter and bill customer usage at fine granularity.
Customer-facing carbon reporting should preserve this and publish emissions per billable resource and reporting period.
Reports should also distinguish energy and capacity components.
Allocation by shared peaks requires demand data for the shared resource pool.
\end{callforaction}

\parag{Reported totals are opaque}
Even at sufficient granularity, a reconciled metric can only anchor to a provider's reported total if that total is itself trustworthy.
Today, customer-facing reports are difficult to reproduce or interpret, making it impossible to distinguish modeling error from reporting methodology~\cite{eilam2021transparent}.

We found this even for AWS EC2, whose methodology is comparatively well documented.
Using standard bottom-up energy models, our estimated emissions were roughly 2$\times$ the reported location-based Scope~2 total at idle and 5$\times$ at moderate utilization, producing a negative residual.
A negative residual is mathematically admissible, but without further information we cannot distinguish model error from differences in reporting boundaries or allocation.
Allocating a negative capacity residual by peak contribution gives larger contributors a larger credit, reversing the intended incentive.

Other providers exhibit similar issues.
In our June~2026 Scaleway tests, daily emissions totals changed when we queried them a week later, and included emissions from before account creation.
Microsoft provides stable monthly reports, but explicitly notes that customer-facing emissions ``will differ from those reflected in Microsoft's corporate disclosure''~\cite{microsoft-s3-2021}.
Without stable, reproducible totals, reconciliation cannot provide trustworthy workload attribution.
Man and Kleppmann~\cite{man2026emissionimpossible} demonstrate how customer emissions reports can be verified automatically at cloud scale without exposing confidential inputs.

\begin{callforaction}
Customer-facing carbon reports should be reproducible and auditable.
Providers should disclose enough intermediate information and methodology for tenants to independently explain reported totals and distinguish reporting assumptions from modeling error.
\end{callforaction}

\parag{There is no common standard}
As we outlined in \S\ref{sec:sota}, providers differ in what they report, how they allocate it, and what they count.
Azure and T~Cloud expose only market-based Scope~2 emissions, leaving no location-based figure to reconcile against.
AWS allocates by usage or equivalent revenue, GCP incorporates list-price weighting, while Oracle, Scaleway, and T~Cloud use hardware energy measurements or power models whose customer allocation rules differ.
Even the hyperscalers disagree on Scope~3 boundaries, including different combinations of upstream fuel losses, buildings, end-of-life, and business travel.

These differences ultimately reflect gaps in today's GHG Protocol guidance for cloud infrastructure, which leaves shared-infrastructure allocation, equipment lifetimes, and several reporting boundaries largely to provider discretion.
\RSCI depends only on the structure of top-down reporting rather than any specific methodology, so improvements to the standard only strengthen the metric.

\begin{callforaction}
The GHG Protocol should define cloud-specific guidance for customer-facing reporting, including common reporting boundaries, allocation rules for shared infrastructure, and standardized outputs.
A standard would make provider reports comparable and enable \RSCI to be portable across clouds.
\end{callforaction}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:future}

In this paper, we outlined a vision for reconciling bottom-up workload metrics with providers' top-down carbon reports.
Our proposed \RSCI framework allocates the residual so that workload emissions sum to the reported total.
Decomposing the residual into energy- and capacity-driven components can preserve operational incentives while attributing idle capacity and embodied carbon to the workloads that drive them.
However, we find that today's provider reports remain too coarse, opaque, and inconsistent to support credible reconciliation in practice.

Future work should evaluate \RSCI on production workloads across clouds, validate allocation weights and their incentives under different residual signs, betas, and provider responses, and aim to build reproducible reconciliation pipelines.
Moreover, we should explore online proxies that estimate residuals and capacity weights from historical reports and load forecasts, and expose the resulting uncertainty, compounded by the inherent uncertainty of embodied-carbon models~\cite{bhagavathula2024uncertainty}, until the final top-down report arrives.
With more granular and transparent reporting as well as clearer cloud reporting standards, \RSCI could make carbon accountability as operational as cost accountability is today.
